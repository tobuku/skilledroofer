Roof Inspection Checklist: What to Look For (2026 Guide)

A complete checklist for inspecting your roof inside and out - whether you're a homeowner doing a seasonal check, a buyer evaluating a property, or dealing with storm damage.

Why Regular Roof Inspections Matter

Your roof is the first line of defense against the elements, and most problems start small. A cracked piece of flashing or a few missing shingles can turn into thousands of dollars in water damage if left unchecked. Regular inspections catch these issues early - before they reach your decking, insulation, or interior ceilings.

When to Inspect Your Roof

  • Twice a year - Schedule inspections in spring (after winter weather) and fall (before winter sets in). These seasonal checks are the backbone of roof maintenance.
  • After major storms - Hail, high winds, heavy rain, and fallen branches can cause immediate damage that worsens quickly if not addressed.
  • Before buying a home - A standard home inspection barely scratches the surface on roofing. A dedicated roof inspection can reveal hidden problems that affect your offer price or walk-away decision.
  • When you notice interior signs - Water stains on ceilings, peeling paint near rooflines, or musty attic odors all warrant an immediate roof check.
  • If your roof is over 15 years old - Aging asphalt shingles deteriorate faster as they near end of life. Annual inspections become critical once your roof passes the midpoint of its expected lifespan.

Exterior Roof Inspection Checklist

Start your inspection from the ground with binoculars, then work your way around the perimeter. If you need to get on the roof, use proper safety equipment - a harness, non-slip boots, and a sturdy ladder secured at the eaves.

Shingle Condition

  • Curling - Edges turning up (cupping) or middle lifting (clawing) indicate age, poor ventilation, or defective shingles. Curled shingles are vulnerable to wind uplift and leaks.
  • Cracking - Thermal cycling causes shingles to crack over time. A few cracks can be patched, but widespread cracking means the roof is near end of life.
  • Missing shingles - Check for bare spots where shingles have blown off. Even one missing shingle exposes the underlayment and decking to water.
  • Granule loss - Check gutters and downspouts for granule buildup. Some loss is normal on new shingles, but heavy granule loss on an older roof means the asphalt is exposed to UV and will deteriorate fast.
  • Blistering or buckling - Raised bumps or wavy lines across the shingle surface usually indicate moisture trapped beneath the shingles or poor attic ventilation.

Flashing

  • Chimney flashing - Look for gaps, rust, cracked caulk, or separation between the flashing and chimney. This is one of the most common leak sources on any roof.
  • Vent pipe boots - Rubber boots around plumbing vents crack and split over time. A failed vent boot is an easy, inexpensive fix - but only if you catch it early.
  • Skylight flashing - Check all four sides of skylight frames for lifted or corroded flashing.
  • Wall-to-roof transitions - Step flashing where a wall meets the roof should be tight and rust-free. Gaps here channel water directly into the wall cavity.

Ridge Caps

  • Inspect ridge cap shingles along the peak for cracking, lifting, or missing pieces. Ridge caps take the most wind and UV exposure of any shingle on the roof.
  • Ensure the ridge vent (if present) is intact and not clogged with debris.

Valleys

  • Roof valleys channel the most water, so they wear faster than flat sections. Look for exposed metal, eroded shingles, or debris buildup that dams water flow.
  • Check for open seams in metal valley flashing.

Gutters and Downspouts

  • Clean gutters and check for excessive granules - a sign of shingle deterioration.
  • Verify downspouts are directing water away from the foundation.
  • Look for sagging, pulling away from the fascia, or standing water in gutter troughs.

Soffit and Fascia

  • Check for rot, peeling paint, animal damage, or sagging sections.
  • Ensure soffit vents are not blocked by insulation, nests, or debris - these are critical for attic ventilation.

Roof Penetrations

  • Inspect around satellite dishes, antenna mounts, HVAC lines, and any other items penetrating the roof surface.
  • Sealant around penetrations dries out and cracks over time. Re-caulk any that show gaps.

Moss, Algae, and Debris

  • Moss - Thick moss holds moisture against the shingle surface and works its way under edges. Remove it carefully and treat with zinc or copper strips to prevent regrowth.
  • Algae - Black streaks (Gloeocapsa magma) are cosmetic but indicate moisture-prone conditions. Algae-resistant shingles help prevent this.
  • Debris - Leaves, branches, and pine needles trap moisture and accelerate wear. Keep the roof surface clear, especially in valleys and around penetrations.

Interior Roof Inspection Checklist

The attic tells you what the exterior cannot. Bring a flashlight, watch your step on joists, and look carefully at the underside of the roof deck.

Daylight Through Roof Boards

Turn off the flashlight and look for pinpoints of light coming through the decking. Any visible daylight means water can get in too. Mark these spots and have them repaired from the outside.

Water Stains and Active Leaks

  • Look for dark streaks, discoloration, or damp spots on rafters, decking, and insulation.
  • Water travels along rafters before dripping, so the stain location is not always directly below the leak source.
  • After rain, re-check for active drips.

Mold and Mildew

  • Black or green spots on wood surfaces indicate sustained moisture exposure.
  • A musty smell in the attic, even without visible mold, signals a ventilation or leak problem.
  • Mold on attic sheathing can affect indoor air quality and may require professional remediation.

Sagging Decking

  • The underside of the roof deck should be flat between rafters. Any sagging, waviness, or soft spots indicate water damage or structural issues.
  • Press gently on suspect areas with a screwdriver - if the wood is spongy, it needs replacement before new roofing goes on.

Insulation Condition

  • Compressed, wet, or displaced insulation reduces energy efficiency and signals moisture problems.
  • Insulation should be evenly distributed and not blocking soffit vents.
  • Check for animal nesting or droppings in the insulation.

Ventilation

  • Ridge vents - Verify that the ridge vent opening is clear from the inside. Insulation or debris can block airflow.
  • Soffit vents - Ensure baffles are installed to keep insulation from covering soffit intake vents.
  • Balanced airflow - A properly ventilated attic has equal intake (soffit) and exhaust (ridge or gable vents). Poor ventilation causes heat buildup in summer and ice dams in winter, both of which shorten roof life.

Condensation

  • Frost or moisture on the underside of the roof deck in cold weather is a sign of inadequate ventilation.
  • Check around bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans - they should vent to the exterior, not into the attic space.

When to Call a Professional

A ground-level visual check and attic walkthrough can catch a lot, but some situations require a licensed roof inspector or contractor.

  • Steep or high roofs - If your roof pitch is 8/12 or steeper, or your home is more than two stories, leave the climbing to professionals with fall protection equipment.
  • Storm damage claims - Insurance companies often require a documented professional inspection. A certified inspector's report carries weight during the claims process.
  • Pre-purchase inspections - Before buying a home, hire an independent roof inspector (not just the general home inspector). They'll assess remaining lifespan and estimate repair or replacement costs.
  • Persistent leaks you can't locate - Water travels along rafters and sheathing before dripping. A pro can use infrared cameras or moisture meters to pinpoint the entry point.
  • Structural concerns - Sagging rooflines, cracked rafters, or widespread decking damage require a structural assessment before any repairs.
  • Your roof is over 20 years old - Even if it looks fine from the ground, an aging roof benefits from a professional evaluation to plan for replacement costs and timing.

How Much Does a Roof Inspection Cost?

Roof inspection costs vary based on the type of inspection, your roof's size and complexity, and your local market.

  • Free inspections - Many roofing contractors offer free inspections, especially after storm events. Be aware that a contractor who expects to bid on the work may focus on selling you a new roof rather than providing an unbiased assessment.
  • Basic professional inspection - $150-$300. Includes a visual examination of the exterior roof surface and attic, with a written report of findings.
  • Drone inspection - $150-$300. Useful for steep or difficult-to-access roofs. High-resolution photos can catch issues not visible from the ground.
  • Infrared / moisture scan - $300-$500. Thermal imaging reveals trapped moisture beneath the roof surface that a visual inspection cannot detect. Often used for flat or commercial roofs.
  • Full structural inspection with report - $400-$500. A comprehensive evaluation including structural integrity, remaining lifespan estimate, and a detailed written report suitable for real estate transactions or insurance claims.

What Affects the Price?

  • Roof size - Larger roofs take more time to inspect.
  • Roof pitch - Steep roofs require more safety equipment and are slower to traverse.
  • Accessibility - Multi-story homes, landscaping that blocks ladder placement, or roofs with limited attic access add time and difficulty.
  • Location - Inspection costs are higher in urban areas and regions with high demand (e.g., after hurricane season).

A $200-$400 inspection that catches a failing section early can save you thousands compared to discovering the damage after water has reached your interior. It is one of the best-value investments in home maintenance.

Frequently Asked Questions About Roof Inspections

How often should you get your roof inspected?

You should inspect your roof at least twice a year - once in spring and once in fall. Additional inspections are recommended after major storms (hail, high winds, heavy snow), if you notice interior water stains, or before buying or selling a home. Roofs older than 15 years benefit from annual professional inspections to catch aging-related issues early.

Can I inspect my own roof?

Yes, homeowners can perform basic visual inspections from the ground using binoculars and from inside the attic. Check for missing or damaged shingles, sagging areas, clogged gutters, and attic water stains. However, walking on a roof is dangerous without proper safety equipment. For a thorough evaluation - especially before a home purchase or after storm damage - hire a licensed roof inspector.

How much does a professional roof inspection cost?

Professional roof inspections typically cost between $150 and $400. Some roofing contractors offer free inspections, especially if they expect to bid on repair work. Drone inspections run $150-$300. Infrared moisture scans and full structural inspections with detailed reports can reach $400-$500. The cost varies by roof size, pitch, accessibility, and your local market.

What do roof inspectors look for?

Professional roof inspectors examine shingle condition (curling, cracking, missing, granule loss), flashing around chimneys and vents, ridge caps, valleys, gutters and downspouts, soffit and fascia, roof penetrations, and signs of moss or algae. Inside, they check the attic for daylight through roof boards, water stains, mold, sagging decking, insulation condition, and proper ventilation. Should I get a roof inspection before buying a house?

Absolutely. A pre-purchase roof inspection is one of the best investments you can make when buying a home. A standard home inspection often only gives the roof a surface-level look. A dedicated roof inspection reveals hidden issues like failing flashing, poor ventilation, or decking damage that could mean a $10,000-$25,000 replacement shortly after closing.
